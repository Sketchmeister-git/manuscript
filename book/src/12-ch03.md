# Nietzsche's Two Roads

*The Last Man and the Übermensch are not two diagnoses. They are two responses to one situation, and the difference between them is a capacity*

Nietzsche is the writer most often invoked in discussions of civilisational decline, and most often invoked wrongly, as a prophet who saw it coming. He was doing something else. He specified the conditions under which a value system fails and the order in which the failure unfolds. A specification of conditions can be checked against a present without anyone claiming its author foresaw that present. That distinction separates a checkable argument from an unfalsifiable prophecy, and this chapter attempts only the first.

Its central claim is structural and, I think, under-noticed. The Last Man and the Übermensch are not two diagnoses of two different situations. They are the two available responses to one situation, diagnosed correctly. Both arise after the guarantee is gone, and both presuppose that values are made rather than found. They differ entirely in whether a person keeps the capacity to make something. Assessed against that specification, the contemporary United States is not mainly on the Last Man's road. The diagnosis that fits is the one Chapter 2 drew from the same author: a nation of lions.

## What died

The madman's announcement in *The Gay Science* is not an atheist's celebration, though it is often read as one. The madman is *seeking* God. The people who meet him with laughter already do not believe, and his message is that they have not understood the size of what they have done. The imagery is of disorientation, not liberation:

> Who gave us the sponge to wipe away the whole horizon? What did we do when we loosened this earth from its sun? … Do we not stray, as through infinite nothingness? (Nietzsche, 1882/1910, §125)

What he reports is a deed, not a discovery: "God is dead! God remains dead! And we have killed him!" (Nietzsche, 1882/1910, §125). Nobody found out that God does not exist. Something was done, over centuries, and the doing was not deliberate.

Nietzsche also identifies who did it. Across §357 of *The Gay Science* and the late works, his claim is that Christianity was destroyed by its own morality, specifically by the will to truth it had cultivated, which eventually turned on its own foundations and found them wanting (Nietzsche, 1882/2001, §357).‡ The tradition's highest value consumed the tradition. That was not a triumph of outside criticism. It was internal and self-inflicted, and on Nietzsche's account it was unavoidable.

What died, then, was not belief. It was the *guarantee*, the assurance that values are found rather than made and are underwritten by something outside human decision.

That distinction does most of the work in what follows, and it is why the usual debate about secularization misses the point. A population can keep its beliefs and still have lost the guarantee, and that is the present situation. Most people still report belief, and almost nobody argues for a value in any currency other than the one everyone else is using.

### The shadows

An earlier section of the same book supplies the image:

> God is dead: but as the human race is constituted, there will perhaps be caves for millenniums yet, in which people will show his shadow. (Nietzsche, 1882/1910, §108)

Nietzsche did not expect a vacancy to appear and be felt as one. He expected it to be occupied by successors that borrow the authority of what died while disowning its source. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries obliged with nation, race, party, History, progress, the market and scientism. Scientism here means not science, which is a method with a self-correcting apparatus, but the position that scientific authority can settle questions of value. Each successor occupied the place of a guarantee, and each was, in Nietzsche's terms, a shadow.

Each has also now been found out in most Western populations, through an ordinary process of institutional disappointment that required no philosophy at all. That point deserves emphasis. The present condition is not the product of anyone reading Nietzsche. It is the product of people watching institutions behave.

## The first road

Nietzsche distinguishes two responses to the discovery, and the distinction is less evaluative than it looks. *Active nihilism* is the destructive stage that clears ground, a strength that attacks exhausted values because it has the power to. *Passive nihilism* is the exhaustion in which the highest values devalue themselves, no answer is sought, and the question is lowered until it stops hurting.

The Last Man is passive nihilism made into a figure, in the Prologue of *Zarathustra*. He has discovered happiness, he says, and he blinks. He has his little pleasures for the day and his little pleasures for the night, with due regard for health. He becomes neither poor nor rich: "both are too burdensome" (Nietzsche, 1883–1885/1909, Prologue §5). Nobody rules and nobody obeys, for the same reason. People still quarrel but are soon reconciled, because otherwise it upsets the stomach. Anyone who feels differently goes voluntarily into the madhouse.‡

The moment that matters is not the description. It is the crowd's response. Zarathustra offers the Last Man as a warning, and the people interrupt him: "Give us this last man, O Zarathustra … make us into these last men!" (Nietzsche, 1883–1885/1909, Prologue §5). They ask for him by name.

That is the sharpest thing in Nietzsche for present purposes, and it is why the Last Man is not a catastrophe that happens to a population. He is a stable equilibrium that a population maintains and would choose again.

His defining phrase, repeated, is *too burdensome*. He is not evil, stupid or deceived. The Last Man has made an accurate calculation about the cost of caring and concluded, correctly on his own terms, that it is not worth paying. Any argument that treats him as simply mistaken has not met him.

## The second road

The Übermensch needs more care than the Last Man. The term has been ruined by a century of misuse, and Nietzsche himself says much less about it. That reticence is significant in itself, not a gap to fill in on his behalf. Four misreadings have to be cleared away.

*It is not a biological or racial type.* The Nazi appropriation ran through Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche's editorial control of her brother's unpublished notes. It was a documented falsification, not an interpretation (Kaufmann, 1974). Nietzsche's own texts are contemptuous of German nationalism and of antisemitism, and his break with Wagner was substantially over them.

*It is not a superior individual in the ordinary sense,* such as a genius or a conqueror. Nietzsche admires many individuals and never identifies any of them as the Übermensch.

*It is not a state one reaches and then possesses.* Zarathustra calls the human being a rope over an abyss, and says that what is lovable in a human is that it is a going-across and a going-under, a transition and not a destination (Nietzsche, 1883–1885/2006, Prologue §4).‡ A reading that makes the Übermensch an achievable status turns it into a possession, which is the thing Nietzsche most consistently opposes.

*It is not mainly a doctrine about other people.* Most contemporary uses of the word rank persons. The text uses it as an orientation for the self, and the orientation is self-overcoming, of what one currently is.

### What it actually names

Three properties remain, and each has a text behind it.

**Creation rather than reception.** The defining act is the making of values. That is only possible once the guarantee has gone, so the project is specifically post-collapse rather than nostalgic. The death of God is the precondition of the Übermensch, not its obstacle. This reverses the usual reading, in which Nietzsche mourns something. He does not. The disorientation of §125 is the price of an opportunity, not the report of a loss.

**Affirmation.** The test is *amor fati* and the thought experiment of eternal recurrence: could you will this life, unchanged in every detail, endlessly? Nietzsche offers recurrence chiefly as a *criterion*, a test of whether a life is one's own, rather than as a cosmology. It is brutal, and it is the exact inverse of the Last Man's calculation. The Last Man asks what is too burdensome. The recurrence test asks what you would carry forever.

**Self-overcoming, continuously.** This means neither perfection nor arrival. The rope over the abyss is not crossed once and finished with.

## The structural point

Here is the claim this chapter exists to make. The Last Man and the Übermensch are two responses to the same situation. Both arise after the guarantee has gone, both presuppose that values are made rather than found, and the difference lies entirely in what is done with that.

The Last Man draws one conclusion: *if nothing is underwritten, nothing is worth the cost, so minimize the cost.* That is a coherent inference. It is why he cannot be argued out of it, and why every exhortation addressed to him fails. His position is not an error.

The Übermensch draws the other: *if nothing is underwritten, creation is both possible and necessary, and I am the only available author.*

The fork, then, is not between belief and unbelief, or between optimism and pessimism. It is between two responses to an identical and correctly diagnosed situation, and the responses differ in whether the person still has the capacity to make something.

This is Fromm's distinction arriving in a second vocabulary. Freedom *from* the guarantee is where both roads begin. The Last Man is what happens when freedom *to* is absent, when the removal has happened and the capacity has not developed. Two philosophers, half a century apart and neither citing the other, describe one fork in two languages. By the rules this book keeps, their agreement is worth noting and is not evidence. The evidence lies in whether the fork predicts anything, and it does.

## Where the trajectory points

This genre usually goes wrong at this point by predicting. The better course is to grade the conditions instead, one at a time.

**The guarantee has gone.** This is not an atheist claim, since a majority still report belief. It is a claim about the background of public argument. The assumption that values are underwritten by something outside human decision is no longer the default, even among believers, who now argue for their values in the same currency as everyone else.

**The shadows have been found out.** Nation, party, progress, market and expert authority have each undergone serial and largely justified disillusionment within living memory. Gallup's nine institutions, tracked consistently since 1979, now average 28 percent confidence ("a great deal" or "quite a lot"). That is near the lowest reading in forty-six years of measurement, and the average has stayed under 30 percent for four years running (Brenan, 2025). The same report adds a qualification. The average is stable only in aggregate. Republicans' and Democrats' confidence swings with which party holds the presidency, and the 11-point gap between them is the largest in the series. Confidence is low overall, and for partisans it follows control of the White House.

**Passive nihilism is widespread.** This is the claim most often asserted and least often shown. Some evidence points toward it: declining participation, declining trust, and a rising expectation that public claims are made in bad faith. But it sits alongside strong evidence of intense, sustained and costly engagement by very large numbers of people. An account that reports only the disengagement is selecting its data, and much writing in this area does exactly that.

**The Last Man's specific signature is present only in part.** The clause about reconciling quickly for the sake of digestion is plainly false of the present, which is conflictual to a degree the Last Man would find intolerable. A figure defined by the avoidance of quarrel does not describe a population that quarrels constantly and at cost.

That last observation decides the matter. The United States is not mainly on the Last Man's road. The Last Man is defined by avoiding burden, dissolving conflict and keeping a contented, blinking, well-regulated peace. The present is defined by an enormous expenditure of energy, sustained conflict, and many people carrying burdens they did not have to pick up.

What fits instead is the other figure from the same author. The energy is real, the refusal is real, and in many cases the courage is real, but almost none of it is construction. The dominant civic act is the No: to a party, an institution, a class of persons, a proposal, a narrative. The lion's competence is at a historical high, and the child's is not visibly present.

The Last Man is a spirit that never became a lion. What is present now is the opposite problem: a nation of lions, superbly equipped to refuse, in a situation that requires construction.

### The lion's stage does not end well by itself

Nietzsche's sequence has the lion pass into the child, but he offers no mechanism by which it must. A lion that cannot make the third metamorphosis has two possible endings. One is exhaustion into the Last Man, when refusal finally costs more than it returns and *too burdensome* becomes true of the fight itself. The other is the reinstatement of a camel's burden supplied by whoever offers one. That is the authoritarian outcome, and Fromm wrote a whole book about it.

Both endings are possible, but the likeliest outcome is neither of them. It is *prolongation*: an arrangement that does not resolve, does not break and does not improve. It is sustained indefinitely because the lion's refusal is genuinely sufficient to block any construction, including the constructions that would be bad. A collapse at least creates the conditions for a revaluation. Prolongation produces nothing but more of itself.

Part Two asks how a population of lions sustains that prolongation without noticing it, and the answer begins with what happens in the second or two between a stimulus and a response.
