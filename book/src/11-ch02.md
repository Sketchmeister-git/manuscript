# The Lion's Problem

*Why a culture can dismantle anything and construct nothing, and why that is a stage rather than a flaw of character*

American political vocabulary is, at its foundation, almost entirely negative. It is a grammar of prohibitions on power. That was a design achievement of the first order, and it is also only half of an equation. When the polity feels a crisis, it reaches for the tool it has, and the tool it has is subtraction.

Nietzsche gave the best available account of what happens when subtraction becomes a permanent stance. He wrote it nearly sixty years before Fromm, in the form of a parable. In the three metamorphoses of the spirit, the camel bears what it is given, the lion wins freedom by refusing, and only the child creates. Nietzsche presents the lion's inability to create as a structural limit, not a moral failing. This chapter argues that a culture stuck at the lion has maximal negative liberty and no capacity to make anything with it. It argues that this describes the contemporary United States better than accounts that reach straight for the Last Man. And it argues that the lion's position was genuinely earned and is not cowardly. The chapter ends by naming the hinge on which the rest of the book turns. The lion was made to fight a dragon with a face, and the authority it now faces has none (a point the end of this chapter develops).

## Freedom from and freedom to

Fromm's distinction between two kinds of freedom is the most useful thing in his work and the most often flattened, so it needs to be stated precisely.

*Negative freedom*, or freedom *from*, is the removal of constraint. It means freedom from the primary ties of family, clan, land and inherited station, and from the authority that told a person what they were. It is genuine and worth having, and modernity delivered it in vast quantities.

*Positive freedom*, or freedom *to*, is the capacity to act from one's own nature, in what Fromm calls spontaneous activity, love and productive work (Fromm, 1941). It is not a quantity of options. It is a *capacity*, and it develops slowly, unevenly and only under supporting conditions.

Fromm's central historical claim is that modernity delivered enormous quantities of the first kind and almost none of the second. It dissolved the structures that told people who they were and put nothing in their place except the market, which is a structure of exchange, not of meaning.

His mechanism runs like this. Individuation cuts primary ties one at a time, and each cut delivers two things at once: an increment of independence and an increment of aloneness. Meanwhile *self-strength*, the actual capacity to act from one's own center, develops slowly. The gap between the freedom a person has and the strength to use it is the standing condition of modern life, and every escape Fromm describes is shaped to fit that gap.

Three consequences follow. Increasing freedom can increase the pressure to escape instead of relieving it. Argument cannot relieve the pressure, because a belief did not produce it. And the gap can be closed from either side, but the two sides are not equivalent. You can reduce the freedom, which every institution of submission has offered and which works, at the cost of the very thing that made the person a person. Or you can increase the strength.

## Reading the Bill of Rights as a grammar

The founding American statement of liberty is a list of prohibitions on government. *Congress shall make no law.* *Shall not be infringed.* *No soldier shall … be quartered.* *Shall not be violated.* *Nor shall be compelled.*

This is not a criticism. The negative form is exactly why the document has lasted. A prohibition on power can be enforced in a way a positive entitlement cannot, the framers understood that, and much of the world's later constitutional practice borrowed the form because it works.

Read it as a grammar, though, and the pattern is unmistakable. The American political vocabulary for liberty is, at its root, a vocabulary of *from*. Nearly the whole lexicon a citizen inherits for talking about freedom is negative in Fromm's exact sense: rights, protections, immunities, limits, checks, government off your back, the right to be left alone.

The matching vocabulary for *freedom to* is thin, borrowed and contested. "The pursuit of happiness" is the closest the founding documents come, and it is a *pursuit* rather than a capacity, a permission to seek rather than a competence to exercise. Everything else in the positive register arrived later, from outside the constitutional tradition, and has been fought over ever since precisely because it does not fit the grammar. That includes the New Deal's freedoms from want and from fear, two of the Four Freedoms that had to be phrased negatively to be heard at all. It also includes the civic-republican language of virtue and participation, and the long argument over positive rights.

This is an interpretive claim. It is offered as the most defensible reading of the record, not as a fact the record forces.

### The consequence is symmetric

A polity fluent in what it must not be subjected to, and inarticulate about what it is for, has a predictable failure mode. When it feels a crisis, it reaches for the tool it has. Look at the shape of American responses to a felt loss of control, across the political spectrum:

- Deregulate: remove the constraint.
- Defund: remove the institution.
- Secede, exit, homeschool, unplug, go off-grid, move to a state that leaves you alone: remove yourself.
- Detox, unsubscribe, delete the account, log off: remove the input.
- Cancel, block, mute, ban: remove the person.

The list deliberately mixes items that belong to opposed coalitions. The coalitions differ on *what* should be removed and agree completely that removal is the form a remedy takes. That agreement is the deep structure, and it is invisible from inside either coalition, because each sees only the other side's subtractions.

Every move on the list is an increment of *freedom from*, and each is defensible in a particular case. Delivered to a person or a polity short of *freedom to*, each one widens the gap. Chapter 1 stated the principle as Proposition 2: subtraction without provision widens the gap it was intended to close.

The claim has a limit, stated so that it is not read as more than it is. Removing a bad constraint is not bad. Some constraints should go, and removing them is an unambiguous good. The claim is that a culture whose only well-developed political move is removal will apply removal when it faces a problem that requires construction, and will then experience the resulting decline as evidence that it did not remove enough. That is a loop.

The historical instances share a structure, and naming the structure is more useful than judging the cases, which would take another book and make this one partisan. A constraint was removed for good reasons. The function it had been performing was not provided for. The vacancy was then filled by something less accountable than what was removed. The pattern recurs in deregulation and deinstitutionalization, in the hollowing of local newspapers and civic gatekeeping, in the removal of editorial control and of professional licensure, and in the weakening of party discipline and the mainline church. Each removal had a real case behind it, each left a function unprovided, and in each something moved into the space that is harder to hold to account than what left.

## The three metamorphoses

*Thus Spoke Zarathustra* opens its first part with "On the Three Metamorphoses": the spirit becomes a camel, the camel becomes a lion, and the lion becomes a child (Nietzsche, 1883–1885/2006). It is the most underused passage in Nietzsche for the purposes of this argument.

### The camel

The camel is the load-bearing spirit. It kneels and asks to be loaded, it wants the heaviest burden, its reverence is real, and its strength is measured by what it can carry.‡ Its governing word is *Thou shalt*. The dragon it will eventually meet is covered in scales, and every scale is a value that has already been declared.

The camel is not a figure of contempt, and reading it as one is the first available mistake. It has genuine virtues: endurance, reverence, discipline and the willingness to be humbled for the sake of something. A culture with no camel phase has no inheritance to work with. That matters because the modern temptation is to believe one can begin as a lion.

The camel's limit is precise. It is strong, and it is not free. Everything it carries was given to it.

### The lion

The lion's task is stated with total clarity. It is to become master of its own desert, to seize freedom, and to say a holy No even to duty. It faces the dragon *Thou shalt* and answers it with *I will*. It fights not for a new value but for the *right* to values.‡ Then Nietzsche says the thing this book is named for:

> To create new values—that, even the lion cannot yet accomplish: but to create itself freedom for new creating—that can the might of the lion do. (Nietzsche, 1883–1885/1909, "The Three Metamorphoses")

Read against Fromm, the correspondence is exact. The lion *is* negative freedom. Its entire accomplishment is the removal of an authority, and that accomplishment is genuine, necessary, hard-won and not to be sneered at. Its power is the power to refuse, and Nietzsche presents the limit on that power as structural rather than moral: it does not extend to making anything.

The lion is the American grammar made into a figure.

### The child

The third metamorphosis is the least quoted and the worst understood. Its terms are peculiar and precise:

> Innocence is the child, and forgetfulness, a new beginning, a game, a self-rolling wheel, a first movement, a holy Yea. (Nietzsche, 1883–1885/1909, "The Three Metamorphoses")

Every term is doing work. Readers who know Walter Kaufmann's translation will remember the last phrase as "a sacred Yes."

*Innocence* does not mean naivety. It means the absence of the resentment the lion's fight necessarily produced. The lion had to fight, and fighting leaves a residue, an identity built from what one is against. The child is what remains once that residue is gone.

*Forgetting* is the capacity to stop carrying the argument with the dragon. The lion is still, in a sense, defined by *Thou shalt*, because *I will* is its negation.

*A game* is activity undertaken for its own sake. Aristotle's *energeia*, activity complete in its own exercise, and Fromm's spontaneous activity arrive at the same place from opposite directions.

*A self-rolling wheel* is a motion whose source is internal. It is positive freedom stated as an image: acting from one's own center, not in reaction to something.

*A holy Yea* is the affirmation that constitutes something, not agreement with what is given. In the same passage the spirit now wills its own will and wins its own world.

On this account, creation is not available to the refusing spirit. It becomes available only after refusal has done its work and been let go.

> **Proposition 3** *(interpretive).* The three metamorphoses are a developmental sequence in which negative freedom is a necessary but strictly intermediate stage. The stage cannot be occupied permanently without pathology, because its defining act, refusal, is defined by the authority it refuses.

## The diagnosis

A culture stuck at the lion has maximal negative liberty and no capacity to make anything with it.

It is not decadent in any trivial sense. It is not weak and it is not stupid. It is strong in exactly one direction. It can dismantle anything and construct nothing, and it experiences each successful dismantling as progress, because refusal is the only competence it has and so the only thing that looks like an achievement.

Nor can it move on to the child. The third metamorphosis requires letting go of the fight, and a spirit whose whole identity is the fight experiences letting go as annihilation rather than growth.

### Why the lion's stage is so hard to leave

This needs to be said sympathetically, because an account that treats it as a failure of nerve has not understood it.

The lion's No was earned. It was won against a real dragon, and each scale on that dragon was a value that turned out to serve someone's interest, as the lion discovered the hard way. The refusal is not a posture. It is a conclusion supported by evidence, namely the record of the last sixty years of American institutional life.

So any invitation to move past refusal reads, from the inside, as an invitation to be fooled again. To a spirit with only two categories, the child's holy Yea looks exactly like the camel's *Thou shalt*. That is the structural reason the American political conversation cannot get past negation. The only visible alternative to refusing looks like submitting, and submission is what refusal was won against.

Nietzsche's answer is that the child's Yea is not a return to the camel's burden. It is a *first movement*, available only to a spirit that has passed *through* the lion rather than around it. He does not say how the passage is made. Part Four of this book takes up that question, and its answer concerns capacity rather than resolve.

### Where refusal is enough

Fairness requires one more point, and the argument is weaker without it. There are domains where refusal is the right and complete answer, and treating the lion as a stage to outgrow would be a serious error in them. Examples are a citizen refusing to take part in a lie, a professional refusing to sign something false, and a person refusing an arrangement that would make them treat someone as an instrument.

These are not incomplete acts waiting for a constructive phase. They are finished acts, and they are the highest thing a person can do at the moment they are required. On Fromm's reading of Genesis, the founding human act is disobedience. The capacity to say no is the faculty that makes a person separate enough to have any other virtue at all (Fromm, 1966).

The claim is narrower. A life or a polity organized around refusal as its *standing* mode cannot construct, and the situations now facing the United States are overwhelmingly problems of construction.

### A test anyone can run

Ask an American what they are against and you will get twenty minutes. Ask what they are for and you will get a negation of something else.

Try it on any contested issue. Pressed for a positive account, the answers on both sides resolve into *not that*: not that regulation, not that curriculum, not that immigration regime, not that platform, not that party, not that elite. The positive content is thin. That is not because people are thoughtless. It is because the vocabulary they inherited has thirty words for removing constraints and about four for building, and all four are contested.

The diagnosis predicts something else that can be checked. A coalition organized around a No should dissolve once the No succeeds, because it has no second act. A polity with a strong positive-freedom tradition, facing the same threats and the same information environment, should show materially less of the paralysis. Appendix D sets out both tests.

## A lion with no dragon

One more step turns this from a diagnosis into the engine of the rest of the book.

The lion's refusal needs a dragon. *Thou shalt* is a command, and a command has an author. Nietzsche's lion is equipped for exactly that kind of authority, the kind Jaynes's bicameral hearer met as a voice and that a child meets in a parent or a worker in a boss. It is overt authority: an identifiable agent issuing an identifiable command, recognisable *as* authority and therefore open to resentment, argument, resistance and disobedience.

Fromm saw that modern authority had changed shape, and he described the change across *Escape from Freedom* (1941) and, most sharply, *The Sane Society* (1955). Beside overt authority there is now *anonymous authority*. It has no agent. It works as common sense, as science, as public opinion, as what one does, as what is normal, as the market, as necessity. Fromm's point is that this is not a weaker form of authority but a stronger one. The boss is replaced by the impersonal requirements of the organization and the father's command by the expectations of one's peers. The person subject to it does not experience themselves as obeying, because there is nobody visible to obey.

> **Proposition 4** *(structural).* Anonymous authority cannot be disobeyed, because it never commanded anything. There is no face to defy, no order to refuse and no relationship within which refusal would make sense. It does not tell you what to do; it tells you what *is done*.

Tocqueville described the shape of this power a century before Fromm named it, at the close of *Democracy in America*. He pictured an immense, tutelary power, absolute, minute, regular, provident and mild. It covers the surface of society with a network of small, complicated rules. It seldom forces anyone to act, but it constantly restrains people from acting. It does not shatter the will; it softens it, bends it and guides it (Tocqueville, 1840/2006, Vol. 2, Book 4, Ch. 6).‡ The rules in that picture are not issued by anyone a citizen could name. They are restraints with no restrainer. Mildness is why it provokes no resistance. Providence is why it is experienced as a service rather than as rule. And because it works as a network rather than through a commander, there is no one to defy.

Now put the pieces together. A culture whose only well-developed competence is refusal faces conditions in which the operative authority cannot be refused. The threats in Chapter 1's inventory share the property that matters: there is no one to refuse. Refusal does not disappear when its object does. It goes looking for the nearest refusable thing, and the nearest refusable thing is usually other people.

Chapter 3 follows the same structure into a second register, Nietzsche's fork between the Last Man and the Übermensch. Part Two asks why the collapse of that search into reflex goes unnoticed by the people it happens to. Part Three shows where the search for a refusable object ends.
